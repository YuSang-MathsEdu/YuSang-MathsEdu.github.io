1. Growing Up Between Borders: Cultural Capacity in Louise Kennedy's Ireland


(A blog post summarised using AI - Copilot | Posted on 2 Nov 2026)
Link to Apple Podcasts Private Passions - Louise Kennedy, writer


Listening to Louise Kennedy speak about her upbringing offers a powerful reminder that culture is not something separate from everyday life. Rather, it is the network of stories, music, language, loyalties, memories, and relationships that enables people to make sense of a difficult world. In reflecting on Kennedy's childhood, we can think of this as a form of cultural capacity, the ability of a community to sustain humanity, imagination, and connection even during periods of social conflict.

Kennedy was born in 1967 and grew up in Holywood, County Down, a few miles from Belfast during the most turbulent years of the Troubles. She has described growing up near Belfast and living through a period when political violence was part of everyday reality.

What is remarkable about Kennedy's work is that she does not write primarily about bombs, political leaders, or constitutional debates. Instead, she writes about ordinary lives unfolding beneath those larger historical forces. This perspective may stem directly from her childhood experience. For many people who grew up in Northern Ireland during the 1970s, daily life continued alongside the violence. Families still gathered, children still attended school, music still played on radios, and communities still celebrated and mourned together.

This is where the concept of cultural capacity becomes particularly useful. A society's cultural capacity can be understood as its ability to preserve meaning and human connection during times of uncertainty. In Kennedy's case, culture provided a way of understanding a deeply divided society without reducing people to political categories.

Kennedy's family background exposed her to the complexities of Northern Irish life. Growing up in a Catholic family near Belfast during a period of sectarian tension, she witnessed the ways identity shaped people's experiences. Yet the world she later recreated in Trespasses is populated not by political symbols but by flawed, complex individuals.

An important turning point came when Kennedy's family moved south when she was around twelve years old, living in Dublin and later County Kildare. This relocation gave her a unique perspective. She was both insider and outsider, shaped by Northern Ireland but growing up largely in the Republic. Such experiences often sharpen awareness of culture itself. When people move between places, they become conscious of accents, customs, assumptions, and identities that others may take for granted.

The experience of leaving Northern Ireland did not erase its influence. If anything, distance appears to have deepened Kennedy's engagement with questions of memory and belonging. Her fiction frequently returns to the emotional landscape of the North, suggesting that childhood places remain powerful even after one has left them behind. As Kennedy has observed elsewhere, places can become frozen in memory long after life has moved on.

What emerges from this story is a richer understanding of cultural capacity. It is not simply access to museums, theatres, or formal education. It is the accumulation of lived experiences, family narratives, local traditions, songs, books, and conversations that help individuals interpret the world around them.

For Kennedy's generation, growing up in Ireland meant inheriting complex histories and contested identities. Yet it also meant inheriting a rich storytelling tradition. Ireland's literary culture has long served as a way of exploring difficult questions that politics alone cannot answer. Kennedy's success as a novelist demonstrates how those cultural resources can be transformed into art that speaks far beyond national borders.

In Trespasses, the characters struggle to maintain intimacy and humanity within a fractured society. That struggle reflects a broader truth about the Ireland in which Kennedy grew up. Despite the divisions of the era, communities continued to create meaning through relationships, language, humour, music, and storytelling. These cultural practices did not eliminate conflict, but they provided people with the emotional and imaginative resources to endure it.

Ultimately, Kennedy's childhood offers a compelling example of cultural capacity in action. Growing up between Northern Ireland and the Republic, between conflict and normality, between memory and migration, she absorbed experiences that later became the foundation of her literary voice. Her work reminds us that culture is not merely what a society produces. It is what enables people to remain human through history's most difficult moments.

In this sense, the enduring achievement of Louise Kennedy's writing is that it transforms personal memory into a broader reflection on how communities survive, remember, and imagine themselves. That may be the deepest meaning of cultural capacity: the power of stories to carry a society through division while preserving its humanity.



2. Born Digital, Thinking Mathematical: Reimagining Mathematics Education in the Age of AI


(A blog post summarised using AI - Copilot | Posted on 2 Nov 2026)


There is a growing tendency to describe today's young people as "digital natives." Yet even this term may no longer capture the reality of the emerging generation. Unlike previous generations who adapted to digital technologies, many children today are born into a world where digital connectivity is not an innovation but an environmental condition. From voice assistants and recommendation algorithms to AI-powered search engines and personalized learning platforms, they inhabit a landscape where intelligence itself appears increasingly embedded in everyday tools. In many ways, they are becoming the world's first generation of digital nomads, moving seamlessly across physical and virtual spaces, learning, communicating, and creating without geographical or temporal constraints.

This transformation raises an important educational question: if artificial intelligence is becoming increasingly capable of solving problems, what kind of mathematical thinking should schools cultivate? More specifically, how should mathematics curricula evolve to prepare learners not merely to compete with AI, but to work alongside it intelligently and ethically?

The Digital Nomads of the AI Era

The traditional image of learning was built around stability. Students attended a fixed location, accessed limited resources, and relied heavily on teachers and textbooks as primary sources of knowledge. Today's learners experience something fundamentally different.

Knowledge is now portable, searchable, and increasingly conversational. A student can ask an AI system to explain algebraic concepts, generate graphical representations, provide instant feedback, or even suggest multiple approaches to solving a problem. Learning opportunities emerge continuously through digital devices, social networks, online communities, and AI-powered platforms.

This does not necessarily mean that young people know more. Rather, it means that their relationship with knowledge has changed. Information scarcity has been replaced by information abundance. The challenge is no longer finding information but evaluating, organizing, interpreting, and using it effectively.

Consequently, education can no longer focus solely on knowledge transmission. Schools must help learners develop the intellectual habits required to navigate complex, data-rich environments where answers are readily available but understanding is not.

Artificial Intelligence and the Changing Nature of Problem Solving

Artificial intelligence is transforming the meaning of problem solving itself.

Historically, mathematics education devoted substantial effort to procedural fluency. Students learned algorithms for arithmetic operations, algebraic manipulation, trigonometric calculations, and statistical analysis. These skills remain important; however, AI systems can now perform many of these procedures instantly and often more accurately than humans.

The question therefore becomes: if machines can execute procedures, what remains uniquely human?

The answer lies not in calculation but in judgment.

AI can generate solutions, but humans must determine:

  • Whether the problem is worth solving.
  • Which assumptions are reasonable.
  • Whether the data are trustworthy.
  • Which solution is most appropriate.
  • What ethical consequences may arise.
  • How uncertainty should be managed.

These are fundamentally mathematical questions, although they extend beyond computation. They involve reasoning, modelling, interpretation, and critical evaluation.

In this context, mathematics becomes less about obtaining answers and more about understanding systems, relationships, patterns, and evidence.

A student who can critically evaluate an AI-generated statistical claim may be more prepared for the future than one who can manually perform a lengthy calculation but lacks the ability to interpret the result.

Why Mathematical Thinking Matters More Than Ever

Ironically, the rise of AI may increase rather than diminish the importance of mathematics.

Mathematical thinking provides a disciplined framework for reasoning. It develops habits of mind that remain valuable regardless of technological advances:

Logical Thinking

Mathematics teaches learners to distinguish valid arguments from invalid ones. In an era characterized by misinformation, algorithmic bias, and persuasive digital content, logical reasoning becomes a civic necessity rather than merely an academic skill.

Decision Making Under Uncertainty

Modern life is increasingly data-driven. Individuals encounter statistical information related to health, finance, politics, environmental sustainability, and personal decision making. Understanding probability, risk, and uncertainty enables informed judgments rather than emotional reactions.

Pattern Recognition

AI systems excel at detecting patterns in large datasets. However, humans still play a crucial role in interpreting the meaning and significance of those patterns. Mathematical thinking develops the capacity to identify structures and relationships across diverse contexts.

Modelling and Abstraction

Real-world problems are often messy and ambiguous. Mathematical modelling teaches learners how to simplify complexity without losing essential information. This skill is central to fields ranging from climate science and economics to engineering and public policy.

Reflective Reasoning

Mathematics encourages learners to justify conclusions and examine assumptions. Such reflective habits become increasingly valuable when AI-generated outputs can appear authoritative despite occasionally being incorrect or misleading.

Thus, developing a mathematical mind is not primarily about producing mathematicians. It is about producing thoughtful citizens capable of making sound decisions in an increasingly intelligent world.

Rethinking the Mathematics Curriculum

If the purpose of mathematics education is evolving, then the curriculum must evolve as well.

A future-oriented curriculum should not abandon foundational knowledge. Numeracy, algebraic fluency, geometric reasoning, and statistical understanding remain essential. However, these foundations should serve broader intellectual goals rather than exist as ends in themselves.

Primary Education: Building Curiosity and Reasoning

At the primary level, mathematics should focus on:

  • Number sense rather than mechanical computation alone.
  • Pattern exploration and mathematical play.
  • Oral reasoning and explanation.
  • Problem solving through multiple methods.
  • Early data interpretation.
  • Computational thinking and sequencing activities.

Young children should be encouraged to explain their thinking, compare strategies, and explore "what if" questions. The goal is to nurture mathematical curiosity rather than merely procedural accuracy.

This is where mathematical games can play a transformative role. Well-designed games encourage experimentation, risk-taking, and persistence without the fear of failure often associated with traditional exercises. Whether through puzzles, board games, logic challenges, or digital simulations, learners encounter mathematics as an engaging intellectual adventure rather than a sequence of disconnected procedures. Games create opportunities for children to discover mathematical ideas organically, building both confidence and resilience.

Lower Secondary Education: Developing Analytical Thinkers

At the secondary level, greater emphasis should be placed on:

  • Logical argumentation.
  • Mathematical modelling.
  • Data literacy.
  • Probability and uncertainty.
  • Digital and algorithmic thinking.
  • Critical evaluation of information.

Students should engage with authentic problems that connect mathematics to real-world contexts. Rather than only solving textbook exercises, they should investigate questions, make predictions, analyse evidence, and justify conclusions.

Mathematical games become particularly valuable at this stage because they create environments where strategy matters more than memorisation. Games involving probability, optimisation, decision trees, or resource management mirror many real-world situations in which individuals must make decisions based on incomplete information. Through gameplay, learners experience concepts such as risk, reward, efficiency, and trade-offs in ways that are both memorable and meaningful.

Mathematics and AI Literacy

AI literacy should not be treated as a separate subject but integrated across mathematical learning.

Students should explore questions such as:

  • How do recommendation systems work?
  • What is an algorithm?
  • How can data produce biased outcomes?
  • What does probability mean in machine learning?
  • How reliable are AI-generated predictions?

Educational games can provide an effective bridge between these abstract ideas and learners' lived experiences. Simulation-based activities allow students to observe how algorithms make choices, how rules shape outcomes, and how small changes in data can lead to different predictions. Such experiences help demystify AI while reinforcing core mathematical concepts.

Upper Secondary Education: Human-AI Collaboration

For older students, mathematics should increasingly focus on:

  • Complex problem framing.
  • Statistical reasoning.
  • Optimization.
  • Decision science.
  • Systems thinking.
  • Ethical analysis of quantitative evidence.

Students should learn not only how to solve problems but also how to collaborate effectively with AI tools, verifying outputs, identifying errors, and applying human judgment.

At this level, mathematical games and simulations can evolve into sophisticated modelling environments. Students might analyse competitive strategies, investigate network effects, explore game theory, or evaluate AI-generated solutions within simulated scenarios. Such experiences shift mathematics from a subject of calculation towards a discipline of strategic reasoning and informed decision making.

More importantly, games cultivate a growth mindset. They reward perseverance, adaptation, reflection, and learning from mistakes, precisely the dispositions learners will need when working alongside increasingly capable AI systems.

From Calculation to Wisdom

The history of mathematics education has often been associated with the mastery of increasingly sophisticated calculations. Yet the emergence of powerful AI systems challenges this long-standing paradigm.

When machines can calculate faster, search wider, and process larger datasets, the competitive advantage of human learners shifts. Success will depend less on performing routine procedures and more on exercising sound judgment, critical reasoning, creativity, and ethical decision making.

Mathematics remains uniquely positioned to cultivate these qualities because, at its heart, mathematics is not about numbers. It is about thinking.

In this context, the inclusion of mathematical games within the curriculum deserves serious consideration. Games represent more than a motivational device. They provide structured opportunities for learners to practise the very skills that AI cannot fully replace: strategic thinking, collaboration, creativity, negotiation of uncertainty, and reflective decision making.

When students engage in a mathematical game, they are often doing much more than applying rules. They are anticipating consequences, testing hypotheses, recognising patterns, adapting to changing conditions, and evaluating alternative courses of action. These are precisely the cognitive behaviours demanded by an AI-rich world.

The most important outcome of mathematics education in the AI era may not be a student who can solve every equation unaided. Rather, it may be a student who can ask the right questions, evaluate evidence critically, reason logically, and make responsible decisions in a world increasingly shaped by intelligent machines.

As our young digital nomads navigate this new landscape, mathematics should become not merely a school subject but a compass: helping them understand complexity, navigate uncertainty, and exercise wisdom in partnership with artificial intelligence.

Perhaps this requires a subtle but profound shift in how we define mathematical success. Instead of measuring only what students can calculate, we must also value how they think, how they reason, how they collaborate, and how they respond to unfamiliar challenges. Mathematical games, puzzles, simulations, and strategic investigations offer powerful vehicles for developing these capabilities because they place thinking at the centre of learning.

In the end, the challenge for educators is not whether AI will change mathematics education. It already has. The real challenge is whether mathematics education can change quickly enough to prepare learners for a future where knowing how to think will matter far more than knowing what to calculate. In that future, mathematical games may prove not to be a supplement to the curriculum but one of its most important foundations, cultivating agile, resilient, and thoughtful learners capable of thriving alongside intelligent machines.
